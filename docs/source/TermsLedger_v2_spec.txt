What changed in version 2.0
New feature: the Debt Desk. When the cash projection shows a gap, the agent sizes it, compares every funding source the SME actually has (credit line, invoice financing, the buyer’s early-payment offer, a government-guaranteed loan, equity as a last resort), and picks the cheapest one that keeps the bank’s covenants intact.
Reframe: a payment-term extension is a loan. The supplier lends the buyer money for free and borrows from its bank to do it. Terms Guard now prices every concession at the supplier’s real marginal cost of debt, which the Debt Desk supplies.
New mandate limits: minimum DSCR, maximum leverage and the credit line’s clean-down rule sit alongside the cash floor and the concession cap.
New capability: capex mode (capital budgeting with financing structure) and a lender-ready borrower file. Sections 7, 5.3 and the new worked examples are new; every other section has been revised.

1. Executive summary
One sentence. TermsLedger is an agent owned by a small supplier that manages both of its debt books — the credit it is pressured to give buyers through payment terms, discounts and rebates, and the credit it takes from banks to survive that — and it can only accept, borrow or commit inside limits the owner signed.
The situation. Large buyers now run AI negotiation agents that message thousands of suppliers at once with packaged offers — early payment for a discount, longer payment terms, rebates on future orders, volume commitments. Lenders now run AI that approves small-business loans in seconds. The SME in the middle has neither: an owner reading a chat message at night, deciding alone whether to accept 60-day terms and, separately, whether to phone the bank.
[CONFIRMED] Pactum’s public material describes Payment Terms, Discount and Rebate negotiation agents operating “within parameters you define”, and reports a Walmart deployment achieving an average 35-day payment-term extension with roughly 68–72% supplier agreement (figures vary between Pactum pages).
[CONFIRMED] On the lending side, MYbank describes a “310” collateral-free SME loan (under three minutes to apply, under one second to approve, zero human interaction) and reports serving over 53 million SMEs by the end of 2023; WeBank’s Weiyedai is an online unsecured working-capital loan that has received applications from over five million MSMEs since 2017.
[INFERENCE] The buyer’s agent decides how long to pay the SME. The lender’s model decides whether to lend to it. Nothing on the SME’s side connects the two, so a supplier accepts terms that create a financing need and then finances it at whatever rate it happens to find.
The reframe in three lines
A payment-term extension is a loan from a small supplier to a large buyer, interest-free and usually with no end date.
The supplier funds that loan by borrowing from its own bank, at a higher rate than the buyer would pay.
TermsLedger prices every concession as the debt it really is, and funds whatever it accepts from the cheapest source that keeps the bank’s covenants intact.

Two features, one engine. Terms Guard (the version 1.0 core) compiles a buyer’s free-text offer into typed obligations, projects them onto the SME’s dated cash trajectory and returns ACCEPT, REJECT, COUNTER or NO DEAL. Debt Desk (new) reads the same trajectory: when it shows a gap, it sizes the gap, compares every funding source on all-in cost, time to cash and covenant impact, and builds a funding plan. Terms Guard creates the need; the Debt Desk prices and fills it. Each makes the other sharper: concessions are priced at the SME’s real marginal cost of debt, and a financing alternative gives the supplier a walk-away option in the negotiation.
The thesis. Every AI-agent wallet control built so far — spend caps, allowlists, revocation, session keys, signer policies, ERC-8196, Cobo Pact, Fireblocks — guards transfers. None of them can see a promise. An agent that agrees to “60-day terms going forward” moves ¥0 today, passes every cap, and commits the SME to carrying ≈¥207,000 of extra receivables indefinitely, funded with debt. TermsLedger is a mandate over promises and over the borrowing those promises cause.
What is genuinely different (and what is not). Nothing here is a new algorithm. Negotiation agents, cash-flow forecasting, loan marketplaces, covenant monitoring and rebate accrual all exist. What was not found in any product is the combination on the supplier’s side: free-text intake of a buyer’s offer; compilation into typed obligations; projection onto the SME’s dated cash trajectory; pricing of each concession at the firm’s own marginal cost of debt; and a hard acceptance boundary that includes aggregate forward concessions and the bank’s covenants. Classification: new application of known mechanisms, with one partial mechanism-level contribution (conservative treatment of contingent promises and uncommitted funding on a liquidity trajectory).
What we keep, add and drop. Kept from earlier work: DueClock’s corrected optimizer (partial early payment plus residual draw), conservative-mode projection, integer-minor-unit arithmetic, the planner/verifier separation, the injection test discipline and the typed harness endpoint. Added in 2.0: the funding menu, covenant predicates (DSCR, leverage, clean-down), the funding router, capex mode and the borrower file. Still dropped: the on-chain policy guard, the lender attestation, the two-machine architecture, and every claim containing the words “proof”, “first” or “atomic”. The lender remains a mocked counterparty; the agent never lends and never signs a loan.
Gate. The project is built only if the validation experiments in Section 14 pass: above all the held-out-offer test (20 buyer offers in natural language, compiled by a form, a bare LLM, and LLM + deterministic checker) and the new router test (the Debt Desk’s funding choice must differ from “always draw the line first” on at least a quarter of held-out scenarios). If the form ties, or the engine’s verdict agrees with a simple per-transaction cap on most offers, TermsLedger is dead and the team builds ClaimGraph-CN (Topic D).
2. Plain-language rundown
2.1 The world it lives in
A packaging subcontractor with forty staff sells to two large appliance makers. It invoices ¥420,000 a month to its largest buyer, which pays in 45 to 60 days. Payroll is due on the 25th. It already carries an equipment loan and a ¥150,000 bank credit line. The supplier is profitable; its problem is almost never solvency, it is whether cash is present on the right dates, and what it pays to make sure it is.
Two new things surround it. The buyer negotiates through software: “We can settle this invoice early at 1.2% if you move to 60-day terms going forward and give us 1% off next quarter.” And money is available from more places than ever — the bank line, invoice financing on a platform, the buyer’s own early-payment programme, a government-guaranteed loan, an online lender. Each phrase in the buyer’s offer has a price, and each funding source has a price. The owner has no tool that puts the two side by side.
2.2 What TermsLedger does, step by step
Terms Guard — the credit the SME gives
Compile the words into money. A language model reads the buyer’s offer and turns each phrase into a typed obligation with a source span: “1.2% early payment” becomes +¥414,960 cash on day 2; “60-day terms going forward” becomes a permanent 15-day delay on every future invoice — an interest-free loan of ≈¥207,000 to the buyer; “1% off next quarter” becomes a future liability of up to ¥4,000 that only exists if that order happens. The model translates. It never decides.
Project the company forward. A deterministic engine runs the supplier’s cash day by day across the horizon under the offer: payroll out, invoices in, discounts, loan draws, interest, repayments, and a parallel forward-liability ledger. It runs in conservative mode: unconfirmed inflows do not count toward hard constraints, a contingent rebate counts at its maximum, and a loan that has not been approved is not cash.
Check every day against the owner’s mandate. The owner signs a small set of limits once: minimum cash ¥300,000, maximum aggregate forward concession ¥5,000 with per-buyer sub-caps, and — new in 2.0 — the bank’s covenants: DSCR at least 1.25×, total debt at most 2.5× annual EBITDA, and the credit line’s annual clean-down.
Act. The agent returns one of four verdicts: ACCEPT; REJECT with the failing predicate and the day it fails; COUNTER, priced from the Debt Desk’s marginal cost of funds; or NO DEAL. Accepted terms become a recorded commitment with an expiry.
Debt Desk — the credit the SME takes
Size the need. Read the gap straight from the trajectory: how much, from which day, and whether it is temporary (one late invoice) or permanent (a terms extension).
Compare the funding menu. Cost every source the SME actually has on the same basis: all-in annualised rate including fees, time to cash, limit, collateral or guarantee, repayment profile, and effect on each covenant. The buyer’s early-payment discount is on the menu too — 1.2% for 30 days is ≈14.8% a year.
Build the plan and stay inside the covenants. Choose the cheapest mix that keeps every projected day inside the mandate, including multi-step plans (“bridge on invoice financing for 21 days, refinance into the guaranteed loan once approved”). Draws and repayments on committed facilities within the mandate happen automatically; anything new goes to the owner.
Prepare the paperwork. For a new facility, assemble the borrower file — cash forecast, covenant headroom, commitments ledger, use of proceeds — for the owner to submit. The agent never signs a loan, pledges collateral or gives a personal guarantee.
2.3 How the two features work together
Buyer offer arrives
   ↓

   ↓
Debt Desk: “Credit line can’t carry it (30-day clean-down).
            Invoice financing can, at ≈10.8% all-in: ≈¥22,456 a year.
            Break-even early-pay discount vs our own financing: 0.88%.”
   ↓
Terms Guard: forward ledger ≈¥26,456 > ¥5,000 cap → REJECT

                      or 60 days with a 0.45% price adjustment
   ↓
Buyer agrees → commitment recorded   |   NO DEAL → Debt Desk funds the gap anyway
Without the Debt Desk, the agent can only say “this offer hurts your cash.” With it, the agent says what the offer costs in real money, how the SME would fund it, whether the bank would still be comfortable — and it can walk away, because it already knows the alternative.
2.4 Why this is compelling
It makes the invisible loan visible. “60 days going forward” sounds like an admin change. The agent shows it as a ≈¥207,000 interest-free loan to a company with a better credit rating than the supplier, costing ≈¥22,000 a year to fund.
It guards the surface wallets cannot see. A prompt-injected negotiation agent can agree to a damaging future concession without any transfer occurring. The demo shows that being caught with a financial consequence on screen.
Financing becomes negotiating power. Knowing its own cost of funds, the supplier knows the most it should ever give for early payment. A known alternative turns “take it or leave it” into a real choice.
It speaks the bank’s language. DSCR, leverage and clean-down are what credit officers check. A supplier whose agent refuses to breach them, and can produce a clean borrower file, is a better borrower — the customer WeBank already wants.
One-sided adoption. The buyer’s agent and the lenders are already there. The supplier adopts alone. There is no marketplace to bootstrap.
It is literally a financial participant. It accepts, counters, commits, draws, repays and refuses on the supplier’s behalf, inside tiers the owner set. It does not summarise a dashboard.
No infrastructure dependency. No bank API, no chain, no second real organisation. The buyer agent and the lenders are fixtures the team writes, with their own hidden reservation values and approval rules.

3.3 Why now
[CONFIRMED] Buyer-side autonomous negotiation is deployed at Fortune-500 scale with published outcome figures.
[CONFIRMED] Lender-side automation is mature: collateral-free SME loans approved by models in seconds (MYbank, WeBank). Money is available; choosing between sources is the SME’s unsolved problem.
[CONFIRMED] China’s National Financial Regulatory Administration said in May 2025 that government-backed financing guarantee institutions charge small and micro enterprises guarantee fees of no more than 1% — a cheap source most small suppliers never apply for.
[CONFIRMED] China’s Order 802 (in force June 2025) and the September 2026 enforcement push make a receivable’s due date a live financial fact; the September 2026 supporting rule remains a draft.
[CONFIRMED] The NFRA’s June 2026 AI guidance stresses accountability, explainability, risk tiering and human intervention at high-risk steps — a mandate-bounded agent with permission tiers and reasons on every refusal maps to that language.
[INFERENCE] Language-model extraction is now reliable enough to compile a bounded offer grammar; deterministic checking is what makes it safe.
4. Innovation thesis
The thesis in three lines
Wallets control what an agent can pay.
Commercial agents create economic promises before any payment exists — discounts, rebates, terms, volume commitments — and each promise is a loan the SME must fund.
TermsLedger controls what an agent can economically promise and how the SME borrows to keep it: compiling language into obligations, pricing them at the firm’s own cost of debt, projecting them onto the cash trajectory, and accepting only inside a signed boundary that includes the bank’s covenants.

5.2 Flow
A buyer offer or a lender term sheet arrives (text, e-mail, portal message).
The compiler proposes obligations or facility terms with spans; schema validation rejects anything malformed or unsupported.
The engine projects cash, liabilities and debt across the horizon under the offer.
If the projection shows a gap, the Debt Desk router costs every committed source and any owner-approved pending source, and returns a funding plan and the marginal cost of funds.
Terms Guard values each concession at that marginal cost; the verifier tests every projected day and month against the mandate, including covenants, and returns a verdict with the failing predicate and step.
On COUNTER, marginal costs shape the counter-offer; bounded rounds; NO DEAL is a first-class outcome, backed by the funding plan.
On ACCEPT, the commitment is recorded with expiry and nonce. Tier-1 operations (draw, repay, invoice-financing submission) run as typed actions in the simulated ledger; Tier-2 operations go to the owner with the borrower file attached.

6.4 What the engine actually optimises
Objective: minimise total cost of liquidity over the horizon — discounts given + interest + fees + a large penalty for any stressed-reserve breach — subject to minimum cash on every day (conservative inflows and committed funding only), each facility’s limit and clean-down, DSCR and leverage in every projected month, the aggregate forward-concession cap and per-buyer caps. In the hackathon this is an exhaustive grid search over a small discrete space (fraction discounted, discount rate, draw per facility, counter-terms); a few thousand candidates in milliseconds; no solver dependency.
Version 1.0 optimum (credit line the only facility): draw the ¥150,000 cap; obtain ¥210,000 net by discounting an invoice face of ¥212,550.61 at 1.2% (discount ¥2,550.61); interest ¥841.64; total ¥3,392.25 versus ¥5,040 for discounting the whole invoice.
Version 2.0 optimum (Debt Desk menu): draw the ¥150,000 line (interest ¥841.64) and finance the remaining ¥210,000 against Receivable A for 30 days (¥1,871.30); total ¥2,712.95 — ¥679.31 cheaper than the 1.0 optimum and ¥2,327.05 cheaper than discounting the whole invoice. Both are the fixture’s optimum under stated assumptions (partial discounting allowed, linear price, compatible timing), not a universal result.
[CONFIRMED (arithmetic)] Every figure in Sections 6 and 7 is reproduced by the team’s fixture script in decimal arithmetic; the 1.0 figures match the three earlier documents within rounding.
6.5 The verdict on the packaged offer
Under conservative mode the forward-liability ledger holds ¥4,000 (rebate at maximum) plus ¥22,455.62 (twelve months of funding the extension on the committed invoice-financing facility): ¥26,455.62 against a ¥5,000 cap. Under 60-day terms the projected cash line falls below ¥300,000 on day 47. The Debt Desk can cure that breach — but not with the credit line, whose clean-down rule rules out permanent use — so the breach is reported as curable at a stated cost rather than hidden. Covenants hold (DSCR 1.92×, leverage 1.72×).
Verdict: REJECT — AGG_CONCESSION_EXCEEDED (¥26,455.62 > ¥5,000); RESERVE_BREACH@47 (curable by invoice financing at ¥1,871.30 a month).
Counter, derived from the Debt Desk’s marginal cost of funds:
Option A: “Terms stay at 45 days, no rebate. We will accept early settlement on up to ¥211,800 of this invoice at 0.85%.” Cost to the supplier ¥1,800.30 + ¥841.64 line interest = ¥2,641.95, just under its own financing cost; 0.85% sits below the 0.88% break-even (Section 7.4).
Option B: “60-day terms accepted with a 0.45% price adjustment on future invoices, no rebate.” 0.45% of ¥420,000 is ¥1,890 per invoice, covering the ¥1,871.30 funding cost, so the net forward concession is about zero. If the guaranteed loan is later approved, the true cost falls to ≈0.22% and the owner can re-price.
The buyer kernel accepts either option if its hidden reservation permits; otherwise NO DEAL, and the Debt Desk funds the near-term gap with line + invoice financing at ¥2,712.95. Walking away is affordable, and the agent knows exactly how affordable.
6.6 Economic corrections carried over from the reviews
[CONFIRMED (arithmetic)] The bible’s original “discount everything, borrow nothing” plan was not optimal; the partial-discount-plus-draw mix is cheaper, and with invoice financing on the menu the discount drops out entirely.
[CONFIRMED (logic)] An additional benefit to the buyer from paying early lowers the discount the buyer needs — the original reservation derivation had the sign backwards.
[DESIGN] The 1.0 counter (“1.5% early settlement, terms unchanged, no rebate”) is withdrawn. Once the SME can finance its receivable at ≈10.8% a year, any early-pay discount above 0.88% for 30 days costs more than its own funding.
[DESIGN] The 1.0 figure of ≈¥1,100 a month for the terms extension assumed the 6.4% line could carry it. The clean-down rule means it cannot; the committed marginal cost is ¥1,871.30 per invoice.
[INFERENCE] A buyer’s supposed regulatory benefit from shortening payables cannot be assigned a convenient yuan value without evidence; the buyer kernel must carry its own declared valuation.
[DESIGN] The horizon must include the loan repayment and later obligations; ending the chart after payroll hides the liability the draw created.
[DESIGN] A small standby line does not repair a failed ¥210,000 early receipt; model the failure branch explicitly and let the verdict become infeasible.
7. Financial mechanism: Debt Desk
7.1 What it is for
Most SMEs do not have a CFO, so financing decisions are made on the phone to the bank when cash “looks low”. The Debt Desk answers three questions every time the trajectory shows a gap, and once more when the owner wants to invest: how much do we need and for how long; which source is cheapest once fees and timing are counted; and will the bank still be comfortable afterwards. It reuses the trajectory engine and the optimizer grid; the new parts are the funding menu, the covenant model and the router.

7.3 Two rules that keep the Debt Desk honest
A facility is not cash until it is committed. In conservative mode, hard constraints count only committed facilities. An approved-in-principle or “pre-approved” loan counts as zero. Pending sources appear in a separate expected plan so the owner sees the upside without the verifier relying on it.
Every source is compared on the same basis. Annualised all-in rate including fees, plus time to cash. A cheap loan that arrives after payroll is not an option for payroll.

7.8 The borrower file
For any Tier-2 facility the agent assembles, from verified state only:
a 13-week cash forecast and the 12-month trajectory under the proposed facility;
covenant headroom month by month, before and after;
the commitments ledger — every accepted concession, draw and repayment, with source spans;
use of proceeds and the repayment source;
the evidence labels for every figure the owner entered rather than the system observed.
[DESIGN] The file is generated, not sent. The owner submits it. “Designed so a lender such as WeBank could consume it; the lender is mocked.”
7.9 What the Debt Desk will not do
It is not a lender and gives no regulated financial advice: it ranks the owner’s own options under the owner’s own limits and shows its working.
It never applies for, signs or guarantees a loan, pledges collateral or issues equity (Tiers 2 and 3).
It does not predict whether a lender will approve; an unapproved facility is zero until approved.
It never takes a rate from an unsolicited message: a lender that is not on the owner-approved menu cannot become a funding source.

9.2 Positioning statement
Buyers have agents that decide how long to pay a small supplier. Lenders have models that decide whether to lend to it. Loan marketplaces help it pick a lender once it already knows it needs one. TermsLedger sits upstream of all three, on the supplier’s side: it prices every concession as the debt it creates, funds it from the cheapest source that keeps the bank’s covenants intact, and says no deal when neither the cash nor the covenants can afford it.
[UNVERIFIED] Absence of a supplier-side product is a search result, not a fact. It must be re-checked in English and Chinese, and the pitch must say “that we could find”.

11.3 Why Topic E and not Topic F
Topic F pays only for real on-chain transactions, and every generic wallet-policy claim is already occupied (ERC-8196 is Final; Cobo, Fireblocks, Openfort and others ship policy-bound agent wallets). Topic E asks for financing and cash-flow support; with the Debt Desk, TermsLedger now answers both halves.

12.2 The two most dangerous silent errors
Authentication is not truth
A schema-valid obligation can still encode the wrong contingency. A verifier that faithfully evaluates a ¥1,000 expected rebate when the owner’s exposure is ¥4,000 will pass every predicate and record a damaging commitment with a clean audit trail. The containment is not more signatures; it is a compiler that must produce a base, a duration and a contingency class for every obligation — or abstain — and a mandate that counts contingent promises at their maximum by default.

A facility is not cash
A funding plan that relies on a pre-approved or pending loan passes every cash check on paper and fails on payroll day when the money has not arrived. The containment: uncommitted facilities count as zero for hard constraints, time to cash is a hard input to the router, and any plan that needs a pending facility is shown only as an “expected” plan beside the conservative one.

16. The pitch
16.1 Ninety seconds
“Walmart’s procurement AI negotiates with thousands of suppliers at once. Its published result: an average 35-day extension in payment terms, with about seven in ten suppliers agreeing. On the other side, lenders’ models approve small-business loans in under a second. The supplier in the middle has a bookkeeper and a chat thread.
Here is what those extra days really are: a loan. Move a ¥420,000-a-month buyer from 45 to 60 days and the supplier is lending it ¥207,000, interest-free, indefinitely — and borrowing from its own bank to do it.
TermsLedger is the supplier’s agent for both debt books. Terms Guard compiles the buyer’s offer into money and projects cash day by day. The Debt Desk finds the cheapest way to fund any gap — the credit line, invoice financing, a government-guaranteed loan — without breaking the bank’s covenants. Together they turn “standard terms going forward” into a ¥22,000-a-year cost, reject it, and counter with a price the supplier can afford. If the buyer walks away, the gap is already funded.
When a message claims a pre-approved loan or a rebate “we agreed last month”, nothing is booked, and the refusal comes with a reason and a source span.
Nothing here is a new algorithm. What is new is the side of the table. Wallets control what an agent can pay. TermsLedger controls what it can promise — and how it borrows to keep that promise.”
16.2 The closing line
“Buyers have an agent that decides how long to pay you. Banks have a model that decides whether to lend to you. TermsLedger is yours.”
16.3 The narrative arc for the hackathon
A real asymmetry with public numbers behind it, on both the buyer and the lender side (why now).
One reframe: a terms extension is a loan the supplier makes and must fund (the insight).
A control gap no wallet closes: promises, and the borrowing they cause (the thesis).
One number that turns “60 days going forward” into ¥22,456 a year, and a counter priced from it (the wow).
The buyer walks and the gap is still funded; the capex loan the bank would refuse is refused first (financing, as Topic E asks).
A refusal under injection with a financial consequence (security, as the rubric asks).
A deterministic engine, honest prior art and a narrow claim (credibility and trust).
